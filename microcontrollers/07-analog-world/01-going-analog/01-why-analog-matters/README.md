# Why Analog Matters

The real world isn't binary. Temperature, light intensity, and sound pressure all vary *smoothly* across a range -- they don't snap between "on" and "off."

Digital pins only see HIGH or LOW, so they can tell you *if* a button is pressed but not *how bright* a room is. To capture those in-between values, your microcontroller needs to read **analog signals** -- voltages that can be any value within a range, not just two extremes.

## True or False

**1. True or false:** A digital pin can tell you the exact brightness level of a room.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**False**: Digital pins only see HIGH or LOW. They can detect whether something is on or off, but cannot measure in-between values like brightness levels. You need analog signals for that.

</details>

**2. True or false:** Analog signals can be any voltage value within a range, not just two extremes.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**True**: Unlike digital signals that snap between HIGH and LOW, analog signals vary smoothly and can represent any value within their range, such as temperature or light intensity.

</details>

**3. True or false:** Temperature and sound pressure are examples of signals that vary smoothly across a range.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**True**: The real world is full of continuously varying quantities like temperature, light intensity, and sound pressure -- these are inherently analog, not binary.

</details>
