# Digital Signals Explained

A **digital signal** has exactly two states: **high** or **low**, on or off, 1 or 0. There is no in-between.

Picture a light switch: it clicks fully on or fully off, never halfway. That is how digital works. Every piece of information a computer sends or receives is built from these simple on/off pulses, strung together in patterns.

## True or False

**1. True or false:** A digital signal can represent three states: high, medium, and low.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**False**: A digital signal has exactly two states: high or low, on or off, 1 or 0. There is no in-between or medium state. This binary nature is fundamental to how digital works.

</details>

**2. True or false:** A light switch is a good analogy for a digital signal because it is either fully on or fully off.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**True**: A light switch clicks fully on or fully off, never halfway, just like a digital signal that is always in one of two distinct states with nothing in between.

</details>

**3. True or false:** Computers send and receive information using patterns of on/off pulses.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**True**: Every piece of information a computer processes is built from simple on/off (1/0) pulses strung together in patterns. Complex data like text, images, and video are all encoded as sequences of these binary states.

</details>

**4. True or false:** A digital signal can be set to any voltage value between high and low.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**False**: A digital signal has no in-between. It is either high or low; there are no intermediate values. This is the fundamental difference between digital and analog signals.

</details>
