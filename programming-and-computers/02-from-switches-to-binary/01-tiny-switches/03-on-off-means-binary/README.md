# ON/OFF Means Binary

Since every transistor is either ON or OFF, we represent those two states as **1** (ON) and **0** (OFF). This two-digit system is called **binary**.

Computers don't choose to use binary: it's forced by the hardware. Transistors can't be "sort of on." They're fully on or fully off, so the math is built around two values. Every number, every letter, every pixel on your screen ultimately comes down to long patterns of 1s and 0s matching the physical ON/OFF states of billions of tiny switches.

## True or False

**1. True or false:** Computers use binary because transistors can only be ON or OFF.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**True**: Binary (1s and 0s) maps directly to the two physical states of a transistor: ON and OFF. The hardware forces the number system.

</details>

**2. True or false:** Programmers chose binary because it's the easiest number system for humans to read.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**False**: Binary is actually hard for humans to read. Computers use it because it matches the physical ON/OFF states of transistors, not because it's convenient for people.

</details>

**3. True or false:** The number 1 in binary means a transistor is OFF.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**False**: It's the opposite: 1 means ON and 0 means OFF. This convention maps directly to electricity flowing (ON/1) or not flowing (OFF/0).

</details>
